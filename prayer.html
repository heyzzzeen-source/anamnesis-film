Synopsis

In a Roman archive, a prayer in Korean survives beneath a signature in Latin. Its writer, Agostino Jeon, asks whoever finds it to say a Hail Mary for his soul. In 1919, Jeon and Antonio Song left Korea under Japanese colonial rule and arrived in Rome the following year to study for the priesthood. Jeon died in Rome. Song fell ill, returned to Korea, and died there. Neither was ordained.

Beginning with Jeon’s prayer, the film traces the two men’s lives through letters, church records, and the places they left behind, from their former seminary in Daegu to a cemetery in Rome. More than a century later, the Hail Mary is heard again in the voices of Korean seminarians and priests. As the prayer passes from the page to living voices, the film asks what a vocation that never reached the altar can leave to those who come after.

Director’s statement

“When Agostino Jeon wrote his prayer, he had no way of knowing who would read it.”

— Changmin Lee

Yet he asked something very precise of that person — one Hail Mary. In 2024, I visited his grave at Campo Verano in Rome. I had known about Jeon and Antonio Song for years. It was after that visit that I decided to make this film.

I once studied for the priesthood in Daegu, at the seminary that grew out of the one Jeon and Song attended. I want to look closely at what they hoped to do with their lives, including the years they imagined spending back home. A letter that Song sent to the rector after his return to Korea is especially moving to me. Already ill, he still hoped to recover and resume his studies. He wrote in the future tense. I want the film to leave room for that future as he saw it.

The camera will spend time in the places where the two men studied and prayed. Alongside their written words, we will hear the voices of people who say the same prayer today.
